The Problem

A state language that the machines speak last

Kazakhstan is building its future on digital public services and, increasingly, on AI. But the large language models behind chatbots, translators and voice assistants were trained overwhelmingly on English. For a citizen who lives, thinks and asks for help in Kazakh, the same tool is weaker, slower and — measurably — more expensive to run.

This is not only a question of national pride or “language development.” It is a question of who gets access. Kazakh is spoken by more than fourteen million people, around 70% of the population10, and the Kazakh-speaking population is growing fast: the 2021 census counted 13.4 million ethnic Kazakhs, 33.7% more than in 200912. Most people are multilingual — 44.9% of residents over five know two languages and 28.6% know three11 — but multilingualism is unevenly distributed: sociolinguists have long found that Kazakh is strongest in rural areas, while many urban Kazakhs use Russian at home1,2.

Globally, only a very small share of the world's more than 7,000 languages is represented in modern language technologies5. Kazakh sits in that underserved majority. A 2025 benchmark of Kazakhstan-specific school and university questions found that even leading multilingual models — Llama 3.1, Qwen 2.5, GPT-4 and DeepSeek V3 — struggle to reach competitive performance in Kazakh10.

So the problem has a social shape: the people most likely to rely on Kazakh — rural, older, less Russian-fluent citizens — are the ones the newest tools serve worst. As the state moves services, information and, soon, AI assistants online, a language gap becomes an access gap.

The social-science question

When a state's core digital infrastructure works best in languages other than its state language, who bears the cost — and does AI narrow the gap between Kazakh-dominant and Russian-dominant citizens, or quietly widen it?

Scope

Who: Kazakh-dominant speakers, especially rural, older and lower-income citizens with limited Russian or English.

Where: nationwide, likely sharpest in rural districts.

Scale: millions of potential users of AI-mediated public services.

Trend: the Kazakh-speaking share is rising while AI is being built into services — the gap matters more every year.

Background & Data

The “token tax”: measuring the gap ourselves

Official statistics tell us who speaks what. They say nothing about how well technology serves each language. To make the gap visible, this project ran its own measurement. AI models do not read words; they read tokens — chunks of text. Models are priced per token, have token-limited memory, and spend compute per token. Researchers have shown that languages split into more tokens pay more for the same message7. How does Kazakh fare?

Method & limits

Six everyday public-service questions were written in English and translated into Russian and Kazakh [translations checked by: your name / native speaker]. Newer tokenizers (GPT-4o) shrink the gap sharply, which shows the tax is a design choice, not a law of nature. Older or open models — the ones a government can actually host locally — carry the heaviest penalty.

What the official data measures

The Bureau of National Statistics census (2021) records self-reported language proficiency, ethnicity and multilingualism by region11,12. Researchers have added the first evaluation resources: KazMMLU, a benchmark of school- and university-level questions in Kazakh and Russian10, and the Kazakh Speech Corpus, about 332 hours of transcribed speech from over 153,000 utterances by speakers of different regions, ages and genders9. On the supply side, ISSAI released the open-source KAZ-LLM in December 2024, trained on over 150 billion tokens in Kazakh, Russian, English and Turkish13.

Where the data is silent

The harder half. Each source answers a narrow question and leaves the important ones open:

Census · medium trust

Proficiency is self-reported

People may over-report Kazakh for identity reasons or under-report it out of insecurity. “Knowing” a language is not the same as preferring it for a medical or legal conversation.

No data

Which language do people use for e-services?

There is no public statistic on the language citizens actually choose on eGov, in call centres or with chatbots — the single number this problem most needs.

No data

Dialects and code-switching

Benchmarks test standard written Kazakh. Rural speech, regional varieties and everyday Kazakh–Russian mixing are largely unmeasured — yet that is how many users will really speak.

Benchmarks · medium trust

Test scores ≠ real-life usefulness

KazMMLU shows models underperform in Kazakh, but school-exam questions cannot tell us whether a pensioner gets a correct answer about her benefits.

Measured here · high trust, narrow

Token counts are exact — but narrow

Our measurement is precise and reproducible, but it captures cost and efficiency, not accuracy or cultural fit.

Who falls through the gaps: exactly the citizens this project is about — rural, older, Kazakh-dominant users — are the least visible in every dataset above.

Current State of Research

Four ways scholars have looked at the problem

Research on this issue comes from fields that rarely cite each other. Click each approach to see what it found.

Known

Kazakh is underserved by global AI; performance and cost gaps are measurable; open Kazakh datasets and models now exist.

Contested

Whether a national model (KazLLM) is worth its cost versus adapting global models; whether technology can revive a language or only reflects existing use.

Missing

Almost no research links AI performance to social outcomes in Kazakhstan — who actually gets worse service, and what it costs them.

The gap this project speaks to: sociolinguists study who speaks Kazakh, engineers study how well machines handle it — but no one has connected the two into a question of unequal access to AI-mediated public services.

The AI-Assisted Solution, and What It Would Cost

Kömekshi: a Kazakh-first public-service voice assistant

Kömekshi (Көмекші, “helper”) is a proposed voice-first assistant that explains public services, benefits and medical or legal instructions in everyday Kazakh — reachable through a messenger app or a phone line, built on an open Kazakh model hosted in Kazakhstan, and always able to hand over to a human.

Run by: a public-service agency with an independent oversight board. Reaching: rural, older and Kazakh-dominant citizens first. Doing: answering questions from official sources only, showing that source, and routing complex cases to a person.

Grounded in two real cases

India · 2023–

Jugalbandi

A generative-AI chatbot on WhatsApp built with AI4Bharat (IIT Madras) and Microsoft. A villager sends a text or voice message; the bot retrieves information on government schemes — usually written in English — and relays it in their own language17. Early on it covered 10 of India's 22 official languages and 171 of roughly 20,000 government programmes17.

Lesson: the model is closest to Kömekshi — and shows that coverage starts small. 171 of 20,000 programmes is a reminder not to over-promise.

India · 2022–

BHASHINI

India's National Language Translation Mission, launched in July 2022 under the Ministry of Electronics and IT, builds a public digital platform for translation and speech technology in Indian languages, so that citizens can access digital services in their own language18. Jugalbandi itself draws on this public language infrastructure.

Lesson: treat language AI as shared public infrastructure — Kazakhstan's open KazLLM and speech corpora are an equivalent starting point.

Verdict

Should Kazakhstan do this? Only under conditions.

The bill is real: a coal-heavy grid, hidden data labour, surveillance risk, and a technology that works worst for the people it targets. But doing nothing has a cost too — Kazakh speakers will meet AI anyway, through foreign tools that charge them more and serve them less. Kömekshi is worth building only if every condition below holds. Tick the ones you think are realistic.

When the answer becomes “no”

If the assistant cannot be shown to be as accurate in everyday Kazakh as a human consultant, if its data flows into surveillance or eligibility scoring, or if it becomes a reason to cut human staff in rural offices — it should not be deployed. A worse Kazakh service delivered by a machine would deepen the very inequality this project describes.
