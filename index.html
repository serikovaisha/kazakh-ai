When AI doesn't speak

Language as digital inequality in Kazakhstan: who pays when the country's most powerful new tools work best in someone else's language — and can AI be part of the fix?

By Aisha Serikova (Opportunity) · measured with GPT-4's tokenizer · ~15 min read

01The problem

A state language the machines speak last

Kazakhstan is moving public services online and, increasingly, into AI. But the language models behind chatbots, translators and voice assistants learned mostly from English. For a citizen who lives and asks for help in Kazakh, the same tool is weaker, slower and measurably more expensive.

This is a question of access, not only of national pride. Kazakh is spoken by more than fourteen million people, roughly 70% of the population10, and the Kazakh-speaking population keeps growing: the 2021 census counted 13.4 million ethnic Kazakhs, 33.7% more than in 200912. Most residents are multilingual11, but unevenly so — Kazakh faded from family life especially in cities4, and many urban Kazakhs still use Russian at home1.

Globally, only a very small share of the world's 7,000+ languages is represented in language technology5. Kazakh is among the underserved: a 2025 benchmark found that even Llama 3.1, Qwen 2.5, GPT-4 and DeepSeek V3 struggle to reach competitive performance in Kazakh10.

So the problem has a social shape. The people most likely to rely on Kazakh — rural, older, less Russian-fluent citizens — are served worst by the newest tools. As services move to AI, a language gap becomes an access gap.

The social-science question

When a state's core digital infrastructure works best in languages other than its state language, who bears the cost — and does AI narrow the gap between Kazakh-dominant and Russian-dominant citizens, or quietly widen it?

02Background & data

The token tax, measured by hand

Official statistics tell us who speaks what. They say nothing about how well technology serves each language — so this project measured it.

AI models read text in tokens: small chunks of characters. They are priced per token, remember a limited number of tokens, and spend computing power on every token. Researchers have shown that languages split into more tokens pay more for the same message7. I wrote six everyday public-service questions in English, Russian and Kazakh and ran them through four real tokenizers.

Beads on a string

Each bead is one token. Red-ringed beads are a single letter the model had to break into several tokens — usually Kazakh letters such as ә, ғ, қ, ң, ө, ұ, ү, і.

Method & limits

Six public-service questions were written in English and translated into Russian and Kazakh (translations checked by the author). Each was tokenized offline with four published tokenizers: OpenAI cl100k_base (GPT-4) and o200k_base (GPT-4o) via tiktoken; Meta's Llama 3 tokenizer — Llama 3.1 is the base of ISSAI's KazLLM 1.013, whose own tokenizer was not tested separately; and DeepSeek's published tokenizer. Method follows Ahia et al.7. Limits: six sentences are a small sample; tokens measure cost and efficiency, not answer quality; translation wording affects counts.

Three spirals, one message

All six questions together. Every dot is a token, laid out along a spiral: the longer the spiral, the more the model must read, store, compute — and charge. Hollow dots are tokens “wasted” on broken single letters.

Model

The newer GPT-4o tokenizer shrinks the gap sharply — proof that the tax is a design choice, not a law of nature. But the open models a government could host at home (Llama, DeepSeek) still charge Kazakh roughly 3×.

What official data covers — and where it goes silent

The Bureau of National Statistics census records self-reported language proficiency by region11. Researchers have added KazMMLU, a Kazakh/Russian benchmark10, and the Kazakh Speech Corpus: about 332 hours of speech from 153,000+ utterances by speakers of different regions, ages and genders9. ISSAI's open KAZ-LLM (December 2024) was trained on 150+ billion tokens in Kazakh, Russian, English and Turkish13. The gauges show how far each source can be trusted for this question.

Known

Kazakh is underserved by global AI; performance and cost gaps are measurable; open Kazakh datasets and models now exist.

Contested

Is a national model (KazLLM) worth its cost versus adapting global ones? Can technology revive a language, or only mirror its current use?

Missing

Almost no research links AI performance to social outcomes in Kazakhstan — who actually gets worse service, and what it costs them.

The gap: sociolinguists study who speaks Kazakh, engineers study how machines handle it — no one has connected the two into a question of unequal access to AI-mediated public services.

04The AI-assisted solution, and what it would cost

Kömekshi — a helper that speaks first in Kazakh

Kömekshi (Көмекші, “helper”) is a proposed voice-first assistant that explains public services, benefits, and medical or legal instructions in everyday Kazakh — on a messenger app or a phone line, built on an open Kazakh model hosted in Kazakhstan, and always able to hand over to a human.

Run by a public-service agency with independent oversight. Reaching rural, older and Kazakh-dominant citizens first. Doing one thing well: answering from official sources only, showing the source, and routing hard cases to a person.

KömekshiPublic-service helper · Kazakh
ILLUSTRATIVE MOCK-UP · SCRIPTED · NOT A WORKING AI

  1. Voice first

    Many target users prefer speaking to typing. Open Kazakh speech data already exists9.

  2. Answers only from official sources

    It retrieves and explains official texts and shows the source — instead of improvising advice.

  3. Human handover

    Benefit decisions, medical emergencies and legal disputes go to a person. It informs; it never decides.

  4. Local and open

    An open Kazakh model hosted domestically: data stays in the country, and foreign APIs' token tax is avoided.

  5. Always labelled as AI

    Kazakhstan's AI law requires labelling of AI-generated outputs16.

The verdict

Should Kazakhstan build it? Only on conditions.

The bill is real: a coal-heavy grid, hidden data labour, surveillance risk, and a technology that works worst for the people it targets. But doing nothing has a price too — Kazakh speakers will meet AI anyway, through foreign tools that charge them more and serve them less. Tick the conditions you believe are realistic and watch the scale.

when the answer becomes “no”

If Kömekshi cannot be shown to be as accurate in everyday Kazakh as a human consultant, if its data flows into surveillance or eligibility scoring, or if it becomes an excuse to cut rural staff — it should not be deployed. A worse Kazakh service delivered by a machine would deepen the very inequality this project describes.

AI transparency statement

How AI was used in this project

What AI contributed: The website's code, layout, and visual design were generated with Claude (Anthropic). Claude also assisted with identifying relevant academic sources, organizing the research findings, developing interactive visualizations, and structuring the website's content. Claude also wrote and ran the script that counted tokens with published tokenizers.

What I verified: I reviewed the academic references, checked the accuracy of key statistics and factual claims against their original sources, and examined the case studies to ensure their relevance to Kazakhstan's linguistic and digital inequalities.

What I accepted: I adopted the four-section structure, the interactive presentation of language-related inequalities, and the proposed AI-powered Kazakh-language assistant as a potential solution. I also incorporated AI-generated design suggestions to make the research accessible and engaging.

What I rejected and why: I avoided unsupported statistics, exaggerated claims about AI performance in Kazakh, and hypothetical examples presented as real evidence. I prioritized evidence-based arguments and clearly distinguished proposed solutions from existing technologies.

Important clarification: The chat demonstration in Section 4 is a scripted design mock-up intended to illustrate the proposed solution. It does not represent actual responses generated by a functioning AI system.
